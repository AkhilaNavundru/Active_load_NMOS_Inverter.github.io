Active Load NMOS Inverter: Performance Analysis

Learn how replacing passive resistors with active transistors improves switching speed, silicon area, and dynamic gain in NMOS circuits.

1. Overview

An Active Load NMOS Inverter replaces the traditional, passive pull-up resistor with an active semiconductor device—typically a depletion-mode NMOS transistor with its gate tied to its source.

💡 Key Takeaway: Integrated passive resistors require massive silicon area. Replacing them with transistor loads shrinks circuit footprint while boosting small-signal gain.

2. Circuit Characteristics

By keeping the depletion load transistor in the saturation region, it acts as a high-impedance constant current source during pull-up transitions.

Small-Signal Gain Formula
A_v = -g_m * (r_o1 || r_o2)

3. Performance Benefits

  • Massive Area Savings: Up to 10–100× smaller silicon area compared to passive poly-resistors.
  • Faster Rise Time: Supplies constant current during output charging instead of an exponential RC decay.
  • Sharper VTC: Higher output resistance results in steeper voltage transfer characteristics.

4. Passive vs Active Load Comparison

Feature Resistive (Passive) Load Active Load (Depletion)
Silicon Footprint Very Large Compact
Pull-Up Behavior Exponential $RC$ delay Linear constant-current
Voltage Gain Low to Moderate High

5. How It Works (In Simple Terms)

Think of an inverter like a water tap and a drain pipe connected to a bucket (the output):

  • The Old Way (Resistor Load): A resistor acts like a fixed, narrow pipe letting water trickle in slowly. When you try to fill the bucket, it takes a long time because the trickle gets weaker and weaker as the bucket fills up.
  • The Smart Way (Active Load): An active load acts like an automatic tap. It stays open wide to rush water into the bucket at full speed, then shuts off smartly when the bucket is full.
💡 Simple Takeaway: Instead of waiting for electricity to slowly trickle through a dumb piece of material (a resistor), we use a smart electronic switch (a transistor) to push electricity through much faster.

6. Why This Was a Big Deal for Chips

Before active loads were invented, computer chips were limited by two main problems:

  • Size: Old-style resistors took up massive real estate on a microchip. Replacing a resistor with a small transistor allowed engineers to pack tens of thousands more components onto the same tiny silicon chip.
  • Speed: Because the active load feeds power smoothly and quickly, gadgets could run at much higher clock speeds without getting stuck waiting for signals to switch from LOW to HIGH.
